Page 1Abstract

TraceableHands is a decentralised application (DApp) built on the Ethereum Sepolia testnet that addresses the trust deficit in charitable giving. Donors contribute USDC stablecoins to milestone-based campaigns. Funds are held in smart-contract escrow and released to vendors in two tranches: 50% automatically upon reaching the funding goal, and the remaining 50% only after a crowd-auditing process in which every donor casts a verifiable on-chain vote. Proof of delivery is stored immutably via IPFS. The system eliminates single points of failure by replacing trusted third-party auditors with decentralised donor governance. The project comprises a Solidity smart contract (OpenZeppelin-based), a React frontend, 22 automated tests, and a full Sepolia deployment.

1   Introduction

Charitable organisations globally raise hundreds of billions of dollars annually, yet a persistent lack of transparency undermines donor confidence. Studies indicate that over 60% of potential donors cite inability to verify fund usage as a primary barrier to giving. Existing solutions such as charity rating platforms rely on self-reported data, introducing the same trust assumptions they purport to solve.

Blockchain technology offers a foundation for verifiable transparency: transactions are immutable, public, and deterministically executed by code. Smart contracts can enforce fund release rules without human intermediaries. However, most existing blockchain charity projects focus only on transparent fund flows; they do not address last-mile delivery verification — confirming that purchased goods or services actually reached beneficiaries.

TraceableHands addresses this gap by combining:

  • Milestone-based escrow that locks funds until conditions are met
  • IPFS content addressing for tamper-proof delivery evidence
  • Crowd auditing — donor-governed voting with three outcomes (Approve, Needs More Proof, Reject)

2   Problem Statement

2.1 The Donor Trust Problem

Traditional charity platforms aggregate donations and disburse funds at their discretion. This creates three failure modes:

Failure ModeExampleRoot Cause
Fund misappropriationFunds diverted before reaching vendorCentralised custody
Unverified deliveryVendor reports delivery without evidenceNo evidence requirement
Opaque auditingInternal audit team with conflicts of interestSingle trusted party

2.2 Limitations of Prior Approaches

Some blockchain charity projects (e.g., BitGive, Alice.si) provide on-chain fund tracking but still rely on a designated oracle or committee to verify delivery. This merely shifts the trust assumption from the charity to the oracle operator. TraceableHands removes this assumption entirely by distributing audit authority across the donor pool itself.

Page 45   Crowd Auditing Mechanism

5.1 Design Rationale

The original design used a pre-registered auditor pool with reputation-gated vote thresholds. While logically sound, it introduced a new trust assumption: the auditors themselves must act honestly and in number sufficient for quorum. The crowd auditing redesign eliminates this by making every donor an auditor for the milestones they funded.

PropertyOld Auditor Pool DesignCrowd Audit Design
Who can audit?Registered auditor pool membersAny donor of that milestone
Skin-in-the-gameNo direct financial stakeDonor's own 50% share depends on outcome
Vote optionsApprove / Reject (binary)Approve / Needs More Proof / Reject
Quorum sourceReputation-gated threshold (1–3)min(3, donorCount)
Re-audit mechanismNoneUp to 3 proof rounds
Trust assumptionAuditor pool is honestMajority of donors honest (by incentive alignment)

5.2 Voting Flow

5.3 Incentive Alignment Analysis

The crowd auditing mechanism is incentive-compatible under rational-actor assumptions:

  • Donors voting Approve dishonestly have no benefit — the remaining 50% goes to the vendor, not to them
  • Donors voting Reject dishonestly recover only 50% of their donation (the other 50% already went to the vendor), making bad-faith rejection a net loss for those donors
  • Vendors submitting weak proof risk a majority "Needs More Proof" vote, which delays payment and costs gas for re-submission
  • Vendors not submitting proof never receive the remaining 50%, which is frozen in the contract

7   Conclusion

TraceableHands demonstrates that blockchain-based crowd auditing is a viable, incentive-compatible mechanism for verifying charitable delivery without trusted intermediaries. By giving voting rights to donors — the stakeholders with the strongest interest in accurate outcomes — the system eliminates the need for a pre-registered auditor pool while improving Sybil resistance (votes require prior financial commitment).

The three-way vote (Approve / Needs More Proof / Reject) is a meaningful improvement over binary audit systems: it allows iterative evidence refinement rather than treating every rejected first attempt as a permanent failure. Combined with the automatic partial-refund mechanism and on-chain IPFS evidence CIDs, the platform provides donors with stronger accountability guarantees than any existing centralised charity platform.

The full system — smart contract, React frontend, 22 automated tests, and Sepolia deployment — was built and tested within the scope of FT5004, demonstrating the practical viability of trustless charity infrastructure on public blockchains.
